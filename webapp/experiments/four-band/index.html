Four bands at once

One Hermes-Lite 2 listening on 10, 15, 20 and 40 m at the same time, on four receivers, from morning into the evening: when each band was open, where its signals came from and how strong they were, and what listening to four bands through one front end did to the radio.

Why: the HL2's four receivers share one filter, one amplifier and one converter. This looks at what that costs each band, against a survey the night before that heard one band at a time on the same antenna. It is a measurement on a computer with WSJT-X's decoder, not something the station does.

Station: one 40 m end-fed half-wave, which is good on 40, 20, 15 and 10 m. A Hermes-Lite 2, gateware 74.2, with the N2ADR filter board; with four receivers it uses the 10 m low-pass for all of them. Receive only.

        How this was measured, and what is left out
        Recording
        tools/hl2_record.py on a computer, the HL2 on its own Ethernet link: four receivers at 48 kHz, each NCO on its band's FT8 dial, one stream an hour, every 15-second slot saved with a manifest line of the radio's counters.
        Decodes
        WSJT-X's jt9 -8 -d 3 on one slot in eight of each band (one every two minutes), each slot converted to 12 kHz by tools/iq_convert 1,500 Hz above the NCO, as the corpus scorer does (tools/decode_bench/sample_jt9.py). A message counts once per slot. The radio's figures cover every slot.
        Stations
        A station is the sender of a decoded message, counted once however often it was heard: the call after CQ (or after CQ and one word such as DX or POTA), or the second call of a TO FROM … message. It counts only when it is a well-formed callsign: a prefix, a digit and a suffix ending in a letter (up to seven characters, for a special-event station), with or without a portable part such as /P or VE3/; a call jt9 resolved from a hash, shown in angle brackets, counts as that call. Every other message goes under unknown or other, counted by messages, not as stations: a sender sent as a hash jt9 could not resolve (<...>), free text, a telemetry message, and any message whose sender cannot be told.
        Grid squares
        The four-character locator a message ends with, drawn at its centre.
        Distances
        Great-circle (haversine) from the centre of the run's four-character grid square to the centre of each heard square, in km (miles alongside: 1 mi = 1.609 km). A square is about 110 by 190 km here, so a distance is rough under about 150 km. A decode is placed at its sender's grid square, the one that sender's own messages carried most often in the run; a decode whose sender never sent one, or is unknown, has no distance. The farthest stations are those heard at least twice on the band, since jt9 -d 3 makes the odd false decode.
        Distance against SNR
        Not every decode drawn: the build tool counts decodes in cells of one distance bin by 1 dB of SNR, per band, and takes the median SNR of each distance bin, which keeps the run's data small.
        Same time of day
        For 10, 15 and 20 m, the four-band side is the 120 slots on the same clock window, in Central time, that the band had in the one-band survey, every slot scored with jt9 and our decoder (tools/decode_bench/score_ab.py). A band without such a window falls back to the sampled jt9 decodes in it, when they cover at least half of it.
        The LNA A/B
        After the four-band day, one receiver on 40 m with tools/hl2_record.py --lna-ab: the LNA held, not stepped, at one gain for two slots, then the other for two, and so on, every slot scored with score_ab.py --arm-field lna_arm_db. Each turn of one gain is paired with the turn of the other after it, which takes the band's drift out of the difference. Clipping per slot is from the run's manifest.
